Sector foundation

Overview and operating model

New Zealand logistics connects an import- and export-dependent economy across long domestic distances, two main islands and a small number of critical ports, roads, rail corridors and Cook Strait links. Operators range from asset-heavy carriers and towing fleets to freight forwarders, contract logistics providers, couriers and warehouse managers. The sector's essential role does not by itself protect an individual operator from price competition, under-utilised assets, customer concentration or disruption.

Common business models

  • Dedicated contract carriage priced by route, vehicle, hour, tonne, pallet or service-level agreement
  • Spot or transactional freight exposed to lane demand, backhaul availability and short-term pricing
  • Third-party logistics combining warehousing, inventory handling, fulfilment and transport management
  • Freight forwarding and customs coordination earning commissions or gross margin without owning all transport assets
  • Towing, roadside assistance and vehicle recovery paid by insurers, fleet managers, authorities or vehicle owners

Industry structure

  • Large integrated national networks coexist with regional fleets, owner-drivers and specialist operators.
  • Scale can improve network density, purchasing, systems and backhaul utilisation, while niche capability and service reliability can support smaller operators.
  • Ports, ferry links, rail terminals, depots and major road corridors create geographic dependencies and potential bottlenecks.

Demand drivers

  • Domestic consumption, construction, manufacturing and primary-sector production
  • Import and export volumes and the location of ports and distribution centres
  • E-commerce fulfilment, service-time expectations and outsourced supply-chain management
  • Population growth, urban density and regional infrastructure investment

Margin and cost drivers

  • Fleet or subcontractor utilisation, empty kilometres, route density and backhaul quality
  • Fuel, road-user charges, tyres, repairs, insurance, labour and lease or finance costs
  • Contract indexation and the speed with which fuel and wage inflation can be passed through
  • Service failures, detention time, claims, damage, re-delivery and outsourced-carrier costs

Cyclicality and seasonality

  • Freight volumes generally follow customer production and consumption, with exposure varying by cargo mix.
  • Retail, agricultural, construction and tourism-linked freight may have pronounced seasonal or project cycles.
  • Network disruption can create temporary demand and pricing opportunities but also significant cost and service penalties.

Regulatory and compliance

  • Transport licensing, driver hours, vehicle safety, weight and dimension rules and road-user charges
  • Health and safety duties for depots, load restraint, fatigue, mobile plant and roadside recovery
  • Customs, dangerous-goods, food-safety or biosecurity requirements where relevant to cargo
  • Emissions transition, resilience planning and customer sustainability requirements

Working-capital profile

Receivables commonly arise from business customers paying after delivery, while wages, fuel, repairs, subcontractors and finance costs are paid continuously. Fast growth can therefore consume cash even when accounting profit rises. Fuel-card terms, customer billing accuracy, proof-of-delivery disputes and subcontractor payment timing materially influence conversion.

Asset intensity

Road carriers, towing fleets and warehouse operators can be asset- and lease-intensive. Freight forwarders may be less asset-heavy but carry counterparty, credit and shipment-liability exposure. Fleet age, maintenance history, utilisation, residual values and whether vehicles are owned, leased or subject to prior security are central to both valuation and lending.

Cash conversion and working capital

  • Reconcile billed revenue to jobs, proof of delivery and customer remittances.
  • Test receivable aging and disputes separately for insurers, major contracts and spot customers.
  • Measure cash conversion after fleet replacement capex, not only EBITDA.
  • Identify seasonal peak funding and any carrier, duty or fuel prepayment.

Collateral considerations

  • Vehicles and material-handling equipment require current valuations, ownership evidence, serial details and prior-security searches.
  • Forced-sale values can be materially below book value, particularly for specialised or heavily used assets.
  • Warehouse inventory often belongs to customers and should not be treated as borrower collateral.
  • A general security agreement may capture receivables but collectability depends on contract set-off and customer quality.

Downside stresses

  • Loss of the largest customer with only partial variable-cost removal
  • Fuel or wage increase not recovered for one pricing cycle
  • Lower fleet utilisation plus a maintenance-capex catch-up
  • Receivable-days extension and simultaneous reduction in supplier terms

Monitoring KPIs

  • Revenue and gross profit by customer and service line
  • Fleet utilisation, loaded kilometres and empty-running percentage
  • Fuel cost and surcharge recovery
  • On-time delivery, claims and downtime
  • Receivable days and overdue debt
  • Maintenance and replacement capex versus plan
  • Net leverage, DSCR and fixed-charge cover

Covenant considerations

  • Minimum DSCR or fixed-charge cover tested after scheduled fleet replacement spend
  • Maximum net leverage with consistent lease treatment
  • Minimum liquidity or borrowing-base headroom during seasonal peaks
  • Reporting trigger for major contract loss, safety event or unplanned fleet disposal

Diligence questions

  • What proportion of gross profit comes from the five largest customers and when do contracts expire?
  • Which costs are automatically indexed and what is the lag before recovery?
  • What fleet is owned, leased or financed, and what replacement capex is required over the debt term?
  • How are job profitability, driver utilisation, claims and proof-of-delivery billing controlled?
  • What assets are available for security and what are their current orderly and forced-sale values?
Business valuation report

Valuation analysis framework

Value drivers

  • Durable contracts with balanced indexation and change-of-control provisions
  • Customer, cargo, geography and service-line diversification
  • Network density, utilisation, service reliability and scalable systems
  • Modern, appropriately maintained fleet and realistic replacement funding
  • Management depth beyond dispatch relationships held by the owner

Multiple influences

  • A contract-led, diversified, capital-disciplined operator generally has lower cash-flow risk than a spot-market fleet with concentrated customers.
  • Asset-light forwarding should be compared on gross profit economics, not gross pass-through revenue.
  • Deferred fleet capex, short leases, customer concentration and owner dependence normally reduce transferability and value.

Normalisation focus

  • Replacement-market remuneration for owner driving, dispatch and sales roles
  • Recurring fleet replacement and maintenance rather than unusually low recent capex
  • Related-party depot rent at market terms
  • One-off accident, claim, relocation or major repair costs only where genuinely non-recurring
  • Lease classification and any pass-through carrier revenue

Peer selection

  • Match asset ownership model, cargo and route economics, contract versus spot mix and geography.
  • Separate freight forwarding, warehousing, road carriage and towing unless the subject has genuinely comparable blended economics.
  • Compare scale, customer concentration, fleet age, margins and capital intensity before using a peer as evidence.

Preferred metrics

  • EV/EBITDA after consistent lease and owner-remuneration adjustments
  • EV/EBITA where depreciation reflects a meaningful economic asset charge
  • EV/gross profit for forwarding only when pass-through revenue makes sales comparisons misleading
  • Free cash flow after maintenance and replacement capex

Discount and premium factors

  • Premium indicators include diversified recurring contracts, network density, strong systems and management depth.
  • Discount indicators include fleet catch-up capex, customer concentration, low backhaul utilisation, claims history and reliance on owner relationships.

Diligence questions

  • Does reported EBITDA fully provide for sustainable fleet replacement and market management costs?
  • How much revenue and gross profit will transfer on a change of control?
  • Which customers or routes create economic profit after all direct fleet costs?
  • Can the fleet and depot footprint support growth without disproportionate new capital?

Market-research narratives

Bank credit paper

Logistics is essential to New Zealand's dispersed, trade-dependent economy, but sector importance should not be confused with borrower resilience. Typical operators face a combination of volume cyclicality, labour availability, fuel and road-cost volatility, network disruption and customer bargaining power. The Ministry of Transport's freight and supply-chain work highlights resilience, productivity, safety and lower-emissions transition as enduring system priorities. For a lender, the relevant question is how those system characteristics flow through the borrower's contracts, lanes, fleet, depots and cash conversion.

Credit analysis should therefore test customer and contract concentration, pricing indexation, route density, empty running, fleet age, maintenance and replacement capex, insurance and safety performance. EBITDA can overstate debt-service capacity if vehicles are being aged rather than replaced. Receivables should be assessed against the faster payment cycle for fuel, wages, road charges and subcontractors, with particular attention to disputed proof-of-delivery, insurer accounts and carrier prepayments. Vehicles may support security, but current ownership, prior liens, condition, specialisation and forced-sale value matter more than accounting book value.

This generic baseline must be reconciled to the subject company. A contracted warehouse operator, asset-light freight forwarder, road carrier and towing business have different working-capital, collateral and margin profiles. The credit paper should name the matched sub-sector, identify where borrower evidence confirms or contradicts the baseline, and convert unresolved matters into diligence requirements, monitoring metrics or conditions rather than assumed mitigants.

Business valuation report

New Zealand logistics spans asset-heavy road transport and towing, lease-intensive warehousing, third-party logistics and comparatively asset-light freight forwarding. Value is driven less by headline industry demand than by the subject company's ability to retain contracts, price cost inflation, maintain network density, convert earnings to cash and fund the assets needed to sustain service. The Ministry of Transport identifies resilience, efficiency, safety and emissions transition as long-term system priorities, while its freight data resources provide context for cargo and trade flows.

For valuation, reported revenue and EBITDA require close interpretation. Forwarders may report large pass-through carrier billings, while fleet operators can report attractive EBITDA during a period of deferred replacement. Normalisation should address owner labour, market depot rent, lease consistency and sustainable fleet maintenance and replacement. Peer selection should distinguish road carriage, warehousing, forwarding and towing, then align contract mix, scale, geography, asset intensity, fleet age, concentration and margins. A generic sector pack does not support a current multiple by itself.

Transferable value is normally stronger where contracts survive change of control, customers and cargo types are diversified, pricing has effective indexation, systems are embedded and management relationships extend beyond the owner. Concentration, short-term spot work, fleet catch-up capital expenditure, claims or safety issues, weak route economics and dependence on an owner-dispatcher should be reflected in forecasts, risk assessment and the selected valuation range only after company-specific evidence is reviewed.

Sector foundation

Overview and operating model

New Zealand construction includes residential and non-residential building, civil infrastructure, specialist trades, maintenance and fit-out. Demand is project-based and regionally uneven. A visible pipeline can support revenue, but profit and cash depend on bid discipline, contract terms, delivery performance, subcontractor capacity, variations, retentions and the timing mismatch between progress claims and project expenditure.

Common business models

  • Main contractors delivering fixed-price, cost-reimbursable, design-and-build or negotiated projects
  • Residential builders delivering contracted homes, developments or alterations
  • Civil contractors performing earthworks, roading, drainage, utilities and infrastructure maintenance
  • Specialist subcontractors supplying labour, materials and technical installation
  • Maintenance and facilities contractors earning recurring or schedule-of-rates revenue

Industry structure

  • The industry is fragmented, with a small group of larger contractors and many regional builders and specialist trades.
  • Projects often rely on multi-tier subcontracting, creating shared delivery and counterparty risk.
  • Public infrastructure, private development and housing cycles have different procurement and funding characteristics.

Demand drivers

  • Residential consenting, household confidence, mortgage conditions and population growth
  • Commercial development economics, vacancy, rents and investor funding
  • Central and local government infrastructure programmes
  • Repair, maintenance, seismic, weather-resilience and compliance work

Margin and cost drivers

  • Tender discipline, contingency, escalation clauses and accuracy of labour and material estimates
  • Variation approval, delay damages, rework, defects and subcontractor performance
  • Site supervision, labour availability, productivity and plant utilisation
  • Material prices, procurement lead times, insurance, bonds and finance costs

Cyclicality and seasonality

  • New-build activity is cyclical and responds to finance availability and client confidence.
  • Weather, holiday shutdowns and consent or design delays can shift revenue and working capital between periods.
  • Maintenance and public infrastructure may be more defensive but are still exposed to procurement cycles and budgets.

Regulatory and compliance

  • Building Act, Building Code, consenting, licensed building practitioner and trade-certification requirements
  • Construction Contracts Act payment, adjudication and retention-money obligations
  • Health and Safety at Work duties across overlapping PCBUs and sites
  • Resource, environmental, insurance, bonding and procurement conditions relevant to each project

Working-capital profile

Construction cash flow can diverge sharply from reported profit. Progress claims are paid after work is performed; retentions, uncertified variations, disputed claims and work in progress can lock up cash, while suppliers and wages fall due earlier. Advance payments and favourable terms can temporarily flatter liquidity and should be separated from sustainable working capital.

Asset intensity

Main building contractors may own relatively little plant but require bonds, guarantees and working capital. Civil contractors can be plant-intensive. Specialist trades vary. Work in progress, retentions and receivables may be economically important but are less reliable collateral than cash or unencumbered equipment.

Business valuation report

Valuation analysis framework

Value drivers

  • Quality, diversity and margin credibility of contracted backlog
  • Disciplined estimating, project controls and early recognition of losses
  • Recurring maintenance or framework revenue
  • Skilled management and delivery capability beyond the owner
  • Strong cash conversion and conservative claims accounting

Multiple influences

  • Maintenance-heavy, diversified contractors with repeat clients generally have more predictable earnings than project-concentrated new-build firms.
  • Backlog is valuable only where funding, terms, delivery capacity and forecast margin are credible.
  • Contingent liabilities, aggressive WIP recognition, plant catch-up and owner estimating dependence reduce transferability.

Normalisation focus

  • Project losses and claims based on latest cost-to-complete rather than historic margin
  • Owner estimating, project management and sales remuneration at replacement cost
  • Sustainable plant replacement and lease treatment
  • Related-party property, plant or subcontract arrangements at market terms
  • One-off disputes only when exposure is resolved and non-recurring

Peer selection

  • Separate residential, commercial main contracting, civil and specialist trades.
  • Match project duration, maintenance mix, contract form, plant intensity, scale and geography.
  • Compare cash conversion, claims policy, concentration and bonding needs before relying on observed multiples.

Preferred metrics

  • EV/EBITDA on normalised through-cycle earnings
  • EV/EBITA for plant-intensive civil contractors
  • Price-to-earnings or DCF where debt and working-capital policy are aligned
  • Free cash flow after working-capital and maintenance capex

Discount and premium factors

  • Premium indicators include recurring maintenance, diverse funded backlog, strong controls and management depth.
  • Discount indicators include disputed WIP, project concentration, thin liquidity, contingent claims and aggressive revenue recognition.

Diligence questions

  • What portion of current EBITDA comes from favourable project timing or unapproved claims?
  • What losses emerge when every material project is reforecast independently?
  • How much working capital and bonding capacity must remain in the business after a sale?
  • Will client, subcontractor and qualified-staff relationships transfer?

Bank credit paper

Construction demand in New Zealand is spread across residential, non-residential and infrastructure work, with materially different regional and client cycles. MBIE's National Construction Pipeline provides a forward view of activity, but a favourable aggregate pipeline does not prove that a borrower's own backlog is contracted, funded or profitable. Lender analysis should distinguish secured work from preferred-bid and speculative pipeline, then test client quality, contract form, delivery capacity, concentration and the latest cost-to-complete.

The central credit issue is cash conversion. Progress claims follow expenditure, while retentions, uncertified variations, disputed claims and work in progress can absorb cash even where reported EBITDA is positive. Receivables may be subject to certification, set-off or defect claims, and protected retention money or customer advances are not necessarily available liquidity. Project-level margin, WIP and claim controls are therefore more informative than annual revenue alone. Bonds, guarantees, defects, related developments and subcontractor failure should be treated as contingent exposure.

The matched sub-sector changes the risk assessment: civil contractors may offer plant collateral but require replacement capex; main contractors may be physically asset-light but carry substantial project and guarantee exposure; specialist trades depend on qualified labour and main-contractor payment; residential builders are exposed to client finance and housing conditions. Conditions and covenants should address actual gaps in project reporting, liquidity and contingent exposure rather than assuming the sector pipeline is a mitigant.

Business valuation report

Construction businesses should be valued on sustainable, cash-converting earnings rather than headline revenue or an untested order-book total. MBIE's pipeline research is useful for framing national and regional demand, while Stats NZ building-consent data can indicate prospective activity. Neither establishes the subject company's win rate, contract quality or margin. Backlog has value only when work is contracted, client-funded, deliverable within capacity and supported by a credible cost-to-complete forecast.

Normalisation requires a project-by-project view of margin, WIP, variations, retentions and claims. Favourable timing, front-loaded billings or unapproved claims can overstate maintainable profit; known losses should be recognised. Owner estimating and delivery roles require replacement remuneration, and plant-intensive contractors need sustainable replacement capex. Peer selection must distinguish residential building, commercial main contracting, civil infrastructure and specialist trades, then align maintenance mix, contract type, plant intensity, working-capital needs and concentration.

Transferable value is stronger where the business has recurring maintenance or framework work, disciplined bidding and project controls, diversified funded backlog, conservative accounting and management depth. Aggressive WIP, one-project concentration, thin liquidity, unresolved disputes, contingent guarantees and dependence on an owner-estimator warrant caution in forecasts and risk assessment. Sector foundation

Overview and operating model

New Zealand manufacturing covers food and beverage, wood products, machinery, metal fabrication, chemicals, plastics, electronics and other specialised production. Many firms compete through niche design, quality, responsiveness and export relationships rather than global scale. Earnings depend on throughput, yield, labour productivity, input and energy costs, pricing, product quality, plant reliability and the working capital tied up from raw material through finished goods and receivables.

Common business models

  • Make-to-stock production serving distributors, retailers or export markets
  • Make-to-order or engineer-to-order production for defined customers and projects
  • Contract manufacturing earning conversion margin using customer specifications or materials
  • Branded food, beverage or consumer-product manufacturing
  • Component, fabrication and industrial-service manufacturing with after-sales or maintenance income

Industry structure

  • The sector is diverse and often composed of SMEs serving specialist domestic or export niches.
  • Scale, automation and procurement can improve unit cost, but flexible short runs and technical capability can support niche operators.
  • Customer qualification, product certification and tooling can create stickiness, while concentration and offshore competition remain risks.

Demand drivers

  • Domestic construction, agriculture, consumer demand and industrial investment
  • Export demand, exchange rates, trade access and overseas distributor performance
  • Customer product cycles, specifications, replacement and maintenance
  • Innovation, sustainability requirements and local supply-chain resilience

Margin and cost drivers

  • Capacity utilisation, batch size, labour efficiency, machine uptime and yield
  • Raw materials, freight, energy, packaging, waste and foreign exchange
  • Pricing lag, product mix, quality failures and warranty or recall cost
  • Maintenance, tooling, certification and sustainable replacement capex

Cyclicality and seasonality

  • Exposure varies by end market: construction materials and capital equipment may be cyclical, while food can be more defensive.
  • Agricultural inputs, export shipping and customer ordering may create seasonal production and inventory peaks.
  • Engineer-to-order operations can show lumpy revenue and work-in-progress movements.

Regulatory and compliance

  • Health and Safety at Work duties for machinery, mobile plant, dust, fumes, noise and hazardous substances
  • Food, product, electrical, building, environmental or export certification relevant to output
  • Resource consents, waste, emissions and energy requirements
  • Product quality, traceability, recall, warranty and importer or supplier duties

Working-capital profile

Manufacturers fund raw materials, work in progress and finished goods before collection. Lead times, minimum order quantities, safety stock, batch economics, customer terms and import content can make growth cash-consuming. Inventory records must distinguish usable, slow, obsolete, customer-specific and consigned stock, while WIP valuation depends on accurate routing, labour and overhead allocation.

Asset intensity

Plant, tooling, property improvements and maintenance are often material. Book depreciation may not equal economic replacement. Specialised equipment can have low alternative-use or forced-sale value despite high replacement cost. Capacity, condition, downtime, compliance and the capital required to sustain output are central to valuation and lending.

Overview and operating model

  • Services compete within local catchments on trust, quality, hours, philosophy, convenience, fees and reputation.

Working-capital profile

Government funding and parent-fee timing can produce a relatively recurring receipt profile, while wages are the dominant frequent cost. Funding adjustments, fee arrears, subsidies, holiday staffing and tax obligations require reconciliation. Enrolment or licensed capacity should not be substituted for actual attendance and billable or funded hours.

Asset intensity

Centre operations require suitable licensed premises, fit-out, outdoor areas, furniture, learning equipment and maintenance. Property may be leased, owned in the operating entity or held by a related party. Leasehold fit-out has limited alternative-use value; freehold property needs a separate appraisal and market rent.

Sector foundation

Overview and operating model

Import distributors source goods offshore, manage freight, Customs and product compliance, hold inventory and sell through business, retail, dealer or direct channels. New Zealand's distance from suppliers and relatively small market can require long lead times and meaningful safety stock. Economic value comes from supplier rights, product knowledge, local inventory availability, channel access and after-sales support, while credit risk centres on foreign exchange, landed cost, stock quality, concentration and working-capital funding.

Common business models

  • Exclusive or authorised distribution of overseas brands
  • Non-exclusive wholesale of consumer, trade or industrial products
  • Private-label importing with local brand ownership
  • Industrial distribution combining stock, technical sales, installation, parts and service
  • Direct-to-consumer import retail alongside dealer or wholesale channels

Industry structure

  • Global suppliers, local distributors, wholesalers, retailers and direct e-commerce can compete in the same product chain.
  • Local scale, stock availability, technical support and established channels can offset limited purchasing volume.
  • Supplier and customer concentration are common, and exclusivity may be territory-, performance- or change-of-control dependent.

Demand drivers

  • Domestic consumption, construction, agriculture, manufacturing and maintenance
  • Product replacement, installed base and after-sales parts
  • Price, exchange rates, brand, availability, service and channel coverage
  • Population, business investment and product regulation

Margin and cost drivers

  • Supplier pricing, foreign exchange, freight, duty, GST, port and storage cost
  • Sales mix, rebates, exclusivity, channel discounts and price pass-through
  • Inventory turns, obsolescence, damage, warranty and returns
  • Warehouse, sales, technical support and last-mile fulfilment

Cyclicality and seasonality

  • Exposure follows end markets and may include consumer, construction or capital-equipment cycles.
  • Shipping schedules, supplier shutdowns, peak retail periods and agricultural seasons can concentrate stock purchases.
  • Currency and freight shocks may move landed cost faster than local prices.

Regulatory and compliance

  • Customs import entries, valuation, tariff, duty, GST, prohibited and restricted goods
  • Biosecurity, food, hazardous substance, electrical, building or other product approvals
  • Fair Trading, Consumer Guarantees, product safety, labelling, warranty and recalls
  • Health and safety duties applying to importers and suppliers of workplace plant or substances

Working-capital profile

The model is commonly working-capital intensive. Deposits or supplier payment, sea transit, Customs clearance and warehousing occur before customer collection, and long lead times encourage safety stock. Growth can therefore increase profit and borrowing simultaneously. Inventory must be assessed by SKU, age, demand, ownership, location and net realisable value, while landed-cost accounting and foreign-currency payables require reconciliation.

Asset intensity

Physical fixed assets may be moderate, but inventory, warehouse systems, racking, vehicles, technical equipment and working capital are central. Stock can support a borrowing base only after eligibility and concentration reserves. Supplier rights, local brand, channel relationships and product expertise can be valuable but are not reliable standalone collateral.
