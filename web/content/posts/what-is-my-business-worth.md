Most owners ask this question for the first time when something has already
started moving. A competitor has made an approach. A shareholder wants out. The
bank has asked for a number on a form.

The honest answer is that a business does not have one value. It has a range,
and where you sit in that range depends on who is buying and why. What you can
do is understand how the range gets built, which puts you in a much better
position than accepting the first figure someone hands you.

## The three approaches

Almost every valuation you will ever see uses one of three approaches, or blends
them.

**Earnings based.** Take the profit the business makes, adjust it to what a new
owner would actually earn, and apply a multiple. This is the common approach for
a profitable trading business and it is the one most buyers reach for first.

**Asset based.** Add up what the business owns, subtract what it owes. This
suits businesses where the value sits in plant, property or stock rather than in
trading profit, and it sets a floor for most others. If a business earns less
than its assets are worth, a buyer will look at breaking it up.

**Cash flow based.** Forecast the cash the business will generate over the next
several years, then discount those future amounts back to what they are worth
today. This is more work and more assumptions, and it handles growth and
investment better than a single multiple does.

A careful valuation usually runs more than one and asks why they disagree. If
the earnings approach says one thing and the assets say something very
different, that difference is telling you something about the business.

## Normalised earnings

The earnings approach sounds simple until you ask which earnings.

The profit in your annual accounts is the profit of your business as you run it,
with your salary, your vehicle, your decisions about when to spend. A buyer
wants to know what the business earns for whoever owns it, which is not the same
number.

Working that out is called normalising, and it usually involves:

- Paying the owner a market salary if they currently take less, or removing the
  excess if they take more
- Removing one-off costs and one-off income that will not repeat
- Removing personal expenses that run through the business
- Adding back interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, which is what
  EBITDA means

Normalisation is where a valuation is won or lost. An owner drawing well under
market salary can look far more profitable than the business really is, and a
buyer will find that in due diligence whether or not you found it first.

## The multiple

Once you have normalised earnings, the multiple decides the rest. It is not a
number you look up. It reflects how risky the earnings look to a buyer, and the
things that move it are mostly not financial:

- **How dependent the business is on you.** If revenue walks out with the owner,
  the multiple falls.
- **Customer concentration.** One customer at forty percent of revenue is a
  risk, whatever the margin looks like.
- **Recurring revenue.** Contracted or repeat income is worth more than the same
  amount won fresh each year.
- **Whether earnings are stable or lumpy.** Three consistent years beat one
  excellent year and two poor ones.
- **The state of the records.** Accounts a buyer can trust reduce the discount
  they apply for uncertainty.

That last one is worth sitting with. Two businesses earning the same money can
be worth materially different amounts because one of them can prove it.

## What you can do before spending anything

You can get a long way on your own.

Pull your last three years of accounts and write down, for each year, what your
salary was against what you would pay someone to do your job. Note anything that
happened once and will not happen again. Work out what share of revenue your
largest customer represents. Ask yourself honestly what happens to the business
if you take three months off.

You now know more about what drives your number than most owners do when they
first sit down with an advisor.
